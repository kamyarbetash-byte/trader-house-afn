# "The Gold That Hid for Fourteen Years" — original script v1 (2026-11-10)
Format: the cartoon-3d-short formula (~60 s, ~3.7 words/s), our own characters + story, English voice, one steady tone.

## Facts (sources)
- Tillya Tepe ("Hill of Gold"), Jowzjan province, northern Afghanistan: excavated 1978-79 by a Soviet-Afghan team
  (Viktor Sarianidi). Six graves, ~2,000 years old (1st c. BC - 1st c. AD), about 20,000 gold pieces: crowns, belts,
  jewellery, small gold figures. (Wikipedia, Tillya Tepe; NEH "Afghan Gold")
- 1988/89: the hoard was moved from the National Museum into a vault of the Central Bank under the presidential
  palace compound (Arg) in Kabul. The vault needed several keyholders ("tawildars"), whose identities were kept secret.
  (Wikipedia, Bactrian gold; Dawn 2004; NEH)
- 1990s civil war: the National Museum was shelled and looted; about 70 % of its collection was lost. Rumours said
  the gold had been melted down or sold. (NPR 2007; NEH)
- 2003: the new government could not gather the keyholders; a safecracking order was signed and the vault was opened.
  President Karzai announced in Aug 2003 the hoard was safe; full inventory with National Geographic 2004.
  (Arab News, Aug 2003; Wikipedia)
- From 2006 the gold toured world museums ("Afghanistan: Hidden Treasures from the National Museum, Kabul").
- Motto carved at the National Museum entrance: "A nation stays alive when its culture stays alive." (NPR 2007; CGTN 2021)

## Script (~205 words)
Twenty thousand pieces of ancient gold disappeared in Kabul, and the people who hid them never said a word.
It's 1978. In northern Afghanistan, diggers open a little mound the locals call the Hill of Gold.
The name turns out to be honest.
Six graves, two thousand years old, packed with crowns, belts and tiny golden rams.
The treasure goes to the national museum in Kabul. Then the wars come.
Rockets hit the roof. Thieves carry off almost everything that's left.
And the gold? Gone. Everyone says it was melted down and sold.
Except it wasn't. Back in 1989, a few quiet bank and museum workers had locked it in a vault under the palace, and each kept one key.
Luckily, nobody knew where the gold was.
Unluckily, nobody knew where the gold was.
You see, a lock with many keys works like a secret shared by a group of friends.
Threaten one friend, and you still only get one key.
For fourteen years, those keyholders said nothing.
In 2003, the new government couldn't even find them all, so they called in a safecracker.
Behind the door, every piece was still there.
Today, the words carved at the museum's door read: a nation stays alive when its culture stays alive.
And the gold that hid in the dark has since toured the world's museums, in the spotlight.
